# Wrapper audit of cleanTMLE 0.5.0

Prepared 2026-10-22 for Andrew Mertens. This is a document of proposals. I read code and did not run any of it, and nothing in the package, the paper or the specs was changed. Paths are relative to the project root unless they start with a package name.

cleanTMLE already hands the targeting of the ATE, the trimmed ATE and the ATT to `tmle`, the time-to-event estimate to `concrete`, the comparators to WeightIt and MatchIt, and the E-values to EValue, and it implements three things itself: the nuisance learners with their cross-fitted super learner (`cleanTMLE/R/learners.R`, 253 lines), the overlap-weighted TMLE (`cleanTMLE/R/fit_candidate.R:76-114`) and the plasmode design stage around them. The three files that hold estimation code are 644 of the package's 3,068 lines of R, so handing all of it to other packages would shrink the package by about a fifth at most, and the plan, lock, dictionary, dossier, simulation and ladder that make up the rest have no counterpart in any package I looked at. I recommend against replacing the in-house fitter with SuperLearner or sl3. The reason it was written still holds (one fit per fold serves a whole boosting or penalty path, every setting's cross-validated risk is kept for the edge check, and copies of a bootstrapped row stay in one fold; `docs/superpowers/plans/2026-10-05-cleantmle-0.4.0.md:24`), and the established wrappers would be slower per setting, would change every design-stage number, and would not make the package smaller in a way a reader would notice. The gaps in reliability are elsewhere. No test compares an in-house component with the established package it stands in for, the versions of the numerical engines are recorded neither in the lock nor in the dossier, one call to WeightIt relies on behavior that the current release on the Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN) has removed, and the edge check keeps counts but not the risks that the plateau test of the supervised-learning skill needs. The sections below give the evidence, the cost and benefit of delegating each piece, what has to stay in-house, the thinnest architecture that keeps the design-stage guarantees, and the smallest experiments that would settle the open learner-defaults questions.

## Cross-fitted super learner

`.settings()` expands the default library into 17 settings: three elastic-net mixing values, each fitted along a 50-penalty path, one fit of multivariate adaptive regression splines (MARS) of degree 2, three neural nets of size 5 at three decays, and ten boosted-tree settings (depths 1 to 5 at rates 0.1 and 0.3, capped at 500 rounds) (`cleanTMLE/R/learners.R:6-12, 27-38`; `plan.R:3`; `test-learners.R:9-14`). `.cv_setting()` fits each setting once per inner fold, reads the whole path of held-out log-loss from that one fit, chooses the best round or penalty, predicts the held-out rows at the chosen point and refits once on the full training set (`learners.R:123-147`). The 17 held-out prediction columns are combined on the logit scale by minimizing the cross-validated log-likelihood over the simplex (`learners.R:159-180`), and `.cross_fit()` wraps this in the outer folds (`fit_candidate.R:13-29`). With K = V = 5 that is 6 fits per setting per training set, so 102 fits per training set and 510 per nuisance. A repetition fits g once and Q once per surface, which is 2,550 component fits for the super learner library on the four default surfaces (`plan.R:239-241`). The first repetition of the final-cohort operators comparison took 827.0 s (`rescueCo/logs/design_stage2_stderr.txt:3`), about 0.3 s per component fit if the main-terms library and the targeting are ignored, and the whole comparison took 1,563.4 minutes (`:6`).

SuperLearner 2.0-42 (CRAN mirror, 2026-09-14; 2.0-29 is installed) takes a library of wrapper functions, builds folds in `CVFolds`, and combines columns with `method.NNLS`, `method.NNloglik` or `method.CC_nloglik`. The second of these fits non-negative coefficients by L-BFGS-B and then divides by their sum, which is not the same as solving the problem on the simplex; the third solves the simplex problem with `nloptr` and is the same optimization problem as `.nnloglik` (`R/method.R`). `CVFolds` groups rows by `id` but refuses stratified folds when an `id` is given (`R/CVFolds.R`), so grouped and stratified folds cannot be had together. The wrappers fix their own settings. `SL.xgboost` fits a fixed number of trees (a thousand by default) with `minobspernode = 10` and records no held-out risk per round (`R/SL.xgboost.R`), `SL.glmnet` runs a 10-fold `cv.glmnet` inside each call and takes `lambda.min` (`R/SL.glmnet.R`), `SL.nnet` has size 2, no decay argument and no input scaling (`R/SL.nnet.R`), and `SL.earth` uses degree 2 and penalty 3 (`R/SL.earth.R`). The skill notes that sl3's `Lrnr_xgboost` stops at 20 trees by default. tmle 2.1.1 lists SuperLearner under `Depends` (its DESCRIPTION), so SuperLearner is already a hard dependency of cleanTMLE. The installed sl3 1.4.5 came from GitHub (`RemoteType: github` in its DESCRIPTION), and from the installed DESCRIPTION files I count 100 packages that it would add to the closure cleanTMLE needs today. lmtp 1.5.4 fits its nuisances with SuperLearner library names, defaults to 10 folds and to the discrete super learner (`lmtp_control(.discrete = TRUE)`), trims density ratios at their 0.999 quantile and not at a propensity bound (`R/lmtp_control.R`), and has no argument for externally fitted nuisances (`R/estimators.R`).

|---|---|---|
| Numerical results | Unchanged, so the 0.5.0 design-stage runs stay valid. | Every design-stage number changes. Fold assignments differ because `CVFolds` draws its own shuffle. The combination is the same problem solved by a different optimizer, with a logit trim whose default I did not see. The wrappers fix different settings (10 observations per leaf where the library sets 0, size 2 with no decay, an inner `cv.glmnet`). |

I estimate two to four times the component fits, from the fit counts; I did not measure it. |

My recommendation is to keep the fitter and pin it with tests. The pieces to pin are `.nnloglik` against `SuperLearner::method.CC_nloglik` on a fixed matrix of held-out predictions, the glmnet path against `glmnet::cv.glmnet(foldid = )` run on the same inner folds and the same penalty sequence (the binomial deviance is twice the mean log-loss), the xgboost path against `xgboost::xgb.cv(folds = )` on the same folds (its mean is unweighted over folds, so compare on equal-sized folds), and the whole ensemble against `SuperLearner::SuperLearner` for a glm and glmnet library on a fixed seed. The ensemble pin needs a tolerance, since the fold draws and the internal tuning differ. In structure the pipeline is the cross-validated TMLE of [@zheng2011] fed by a super learner [@vanderlaanSuperLearner2007]; I did not re-read zheng2011 to confirm that the pooled fluctuation matches.

## Overlap-weighted TMLE

`.target_ato()` alternates a logistic fluctuation of the outcome regression along `A(1 - g) - (1 - A)g` with a logistic fluctuation of the propensity score along `(Q1 - Q0 - psi)(1 - 2g)`, for at most 20 iterations, until the means of the two influence-curve components fall below `sd / (sqrt(n) log n)`; the standard error is the standard deviation of the efficient influence function over the square root of n (`fit_candidate.R:76-114`). It clips g and Q at one in a million (`:89, 98-105`) and has no truncation level, so `.fit_and_target()` computes the ATO once and copies the row across the plan's truncation levels (`:179-182`). The manuscript already says that the ATO has no truncation level and cites [@lee2026] (`manuscript/methods.qmd:114`).

I found no overlap-weighted TMLE in tmle 2.1.1, whose `tmle()` has no estimand argument and returns the ATE, the ATT and the average effect among the controls (`R/tmle.R`); in the exports of tmle3 0.2.0 (`Param_ATE`, `Param_ATT`, `Param_ATC`, `Param_MSM`, `Param_TSM`, `Param_stratified`, `Param_survival`, and specifications for the odds ratio, the relative risk and attributable risk); or in lmtp 1.5.4, which is built on shift interventions. WeightIt supplies overlap weights for the non-targeted estimator (`get_w_from_ps(estimand = "ATO")`, WeightIt 2.1.0). The reference in the project's bibliography is [@lee2026] (Biometrical Journal). The publisher's page returned 403, so I could not read it, and I have not verified that `.target_ato()` is the estimator of that paper or whether the paper comes with software.

| | Keep in-house | Wait for a package |
|---|---|---|
| Numerical results | Unchanged. | Unknown; a package would bring its own bounds and stopping rule. |
| Runtime | Negligible against the nuisance fits. | Not applicable. |
| Dependencies | None. | One more, if one appears. |
| Maintenance | 39 lines, with a test against an augmented estimator and a Monte Carlo truth (`test-fit-candidate.R:39-55`). | Not applicable. |

My recommendation is to keep it, with two more pins. The first is a coverage check on a known-truth data set with the true g and Q supplied, which separates the influence-curve variance from nuisance estimation (the existing test checks the point estimate only). The second is a sentence in the dossier and the vignettes saying that truncation levels do not apply to the ATO, because a reader who sees `sl_t0.05` as the selected ATO candidate may think the score was truncated. If [@lee2026] does not derive the efficient influence function and the two-step targeting, add [cite: the efficient influence function of the overlap-weighted effect and its two-step targeting].

## The ATT refit workaround

`.tmle_args()` passes the cross-fitted g and Q to `tmle::tmle` with `gbound = c(truncation, 1 - truncation)` and `g.SL.library = "SL.glm"`, and sets `evalATT = FALSE` when the ATT is not the target (`fit_candidate.R:48-61`). The comment at `:49-52` says why: tmle refits a treatment model for the ATT when more than a tenth of the controls fall outside the overlap subset, whatever `g1W` it is given.

In `tmle()` (tmle 2.1.1, `R/tmle.R`) the ATT block keeps the rows whose g is at least the smallest g among the treated, and it uses all rows and the original g when ten or fewer controls or treated would remain. If at least 90% of the controls are kept, it re-estimates only an intercept on the logit scale. Otherwise it calls `estimateG` again with `g1W = NULL`, using `g.SL.library`, with the cross-fitted score as an extra covariate beside `W[, retainW.A]`. The ATT bound is `c(min(gbound), 1)`, and the variance is `var(IC)/n` over the kept rows. The tmle release notes say that the trimming of controls came in 2.0.1 to improve finite-sample bias and variance, and that 2.1.1 added `evalATT` and changed two defaults, one of them how g enters the fluctuation. The ATT convention, the existence of `evalATT` and the default targeting therefore depend on the tmle version, and the package's DESCRIPTION sets no floor on it (`cleanTMLE/DESCRIPTION:28`). The guard `"evalATT" %in% names(formals(tmle::tmle))` at `fit_candidate.R:59` would let an older tmle run silently with other numbers.

Two further facts come from the same file. The covariates `retainW` are chosen by a screening step, `.prescreenW.g`, that runs whenever `prescreenW.g` is on (the default) and no formula is given. As far as I can tell from the excerpt I read, the code does not test whether `g1W` and `Q` were supplied, and `retainW` matters only to the ATT refit; if so, every `tmle()` call from cleanTMLE pays for the screening, including the ATE and trimmed-ATE calls where its result is never used. And a scalar `gbound` would give the ATE a one-sided bound (the length-1 branch sets the upper bound to 1), so the length-2 vector at `fit_candidate.R:54` is a convention that a test should pin.

| | Keep delegating, with the workaround | Target the ATT in-house |
|---|---|---|
| Numerical results | Unchanged; the manuscript already describes the refit (`manuscript/methods.qmd:112`). | Changes the estimate and the standard error wherever controls lie below the treated support, because tmle's trimming would be gone. |
| Runtime | Can be cut for non-ATT calls with `prescreenW.g = FALSE`, which should leave the ATE and trimmed ATE unchanged because `retainW` is not used there (inference from the code; a profile and a pin settle it). | About 25 lines, and probably faster than a tmle call (my estimate). |
| Dependencies | tmle stays. | tmle stays for the ATE. |
| Maintenance | Rests on tmle internals (the 10% rule, the trimming, `evalATT`); a version floor and a pin contain it. | Our code, our bugs, and a departure from the established ATT. |

My recommendation is to keep delegating and to add a version floor and pins. Require `tmle (>= 2.1.1)` and delete the `formals` guard. Pin the ATT against a direct `tmle::tmle` call in both regimes, a sample with few controls below the treated support and one with more than a tenth. Compute and report, outside tmle, whether the refit fired (the share of controls with g below the smallest treated g, against 0.1), which is the wishlist item on stating it in the dossier (`cleanTMLE/WISHLIST.md:43-44`). When the refit fires, the ATT uses an in-sample logistic regression and not the plan's `ps_library`. The simulation and the analysis make the same substitution, so the guarantee of one fitter holds, but the reader should be told.

## Weighting and matching comparators

`.estimate_weighting()` calls `WeightIt::weightit(method = "glm")` and `lm_weightit(weightit = w)` (`estimate_effect.R:39-40`), and for the trimmed ATE it first fits the cross-fitted score in-house to define the population (`:34-35`). `.estimate_matching()` calls `MatchIt::matchit` for the ATT with a 0.2 caliper and then `WeightIt::lm_weightit(.Y ~ .A, data = md, weights = weights, cluster = ~subclass)` (`:60-68`; `cleanTMLE-package.R:4-7` lists `weights` as a global variable for this call).

The WeightIt 2.1.0 source on the CRAN mirror (2026-09-30; 1.3.2 is installed) documents `weights` as not allowed in `lm_weightit()`, and the code warns and removes it, telling the user to supply a `weightit` object (`R/glm_weightit.R`). The WeightIt release notes put this change in 2.0.0. The matching call therefore ignores its weights under the current CRAN release, and DESCRIPTION sets no floor on WeightIt. With the settings the package uses (one-to-one matching without replacement, from my reading of the MatchIt defaults, which I did not verify here) the weights are all 1, so the estimate may not change, but the call warns and breaks as soon as any matching option is exposed. WeightIt 2.1.0 also requires cobalt 5.0.0 or later and ggplot2 4.0.0 or later (its DESCRIPTION), so a fresh install changes the dependency closure. WeightIt exports `get_w_from_ps()` and `ESS()` in both versions I looked at (the 1.3.2 NAMESPACE and the 2.1.0 source). Its overlap weights equal the ones in `.estimand_weights()` (`assess_design.R:1-7`) by formula, but it has no trimmed-ATE weights and does not truncate (`R/get_w_from_ps.R`).

| | Keep delegating, in Suggests | Keep as now, in Imports |
|---|---|---|
| Numerical results | Unchanged once the matching call is fixed. | Matching weights silently dropped on WeightIt 2.x. |
| Runtime | Not relevant; about a second per data set (`simulation/PROPOSAL-tmle-comparison-2026-10-20.md:60`). | Same. |
| Dependencies | Moving WeightIt and cobalt out of Imports removes 7 packages from the hard closure (52 to 45, counted from installed DESCRIPTION files); moving ggplot2 as well removes 15 more. | No change. |
| Maintenance | `requireNamespace` guards, as MatchIt already has (`estimate_effect.R:52`), and a version guard. | Two fast-moving packages are hard dependencies of a design tool. |

My recommendation is to keep the delegation, fix the call, move the packages to Suggests and pin the diagnostics. The only use of cobalt is the standardized difference in `assess_design.R:125`, which is a few lines of base R. If Andrew accepts the parsimony audit's proposal to cut the comparators (`audit/2026-10-22/01-parsimony.md`, the rows for `.estimate_weighting` and `.estimate_matching`), the dependency question disappears; but the simulation proposal compares TMLE with weighting and matching "as the package implements it" (`simulation/PROPOSAL-tmle-comparison-2026-10-20.md:30-31`), so that decision should come before either change. The pin for the diagnostics is a test that `.estimand_weights()` and `.ess()` equal `WeightIt::get_w_from_ps()` and `WeightIt::ESS()` for the ATE, the ATT and the ATO, skipped when WeightIt is absent.

## Propensity truncation and bounding

The package bounds values in six places, and they follow different conventions.

| Where | Convention | Established package |
|---|---|---|
| Learner predictions, combination and fitted g | Clipped to one in a million from 0 and 1 (`learners.R:99, 160, 210`) | SuperLearner trims on the logit scale with `control$trimLogit` (`R/method.R`; default not seen) |
| Targeting of ATE, trimmed ATE | `gbound = c(t, 1 - t)`, two-sided (`fit_candidate.R:54`) | tmle: a vector is used as given, a scalar gives a one-sided bound (`R/tmle.R`) |
| Targeting of ATT | tmle bound `c(min(gbound), 1)` after the optional refit | tmle (`R/tmle.R`) |
| Outcome regression passed to tmle | Clipped by tmle to about 0.0005 and 0.9995 (`alpha = 0.9995`) | tmle (`R/tmle.R`, `.initStage1` and `estimateQ`) |
| Targeting of ATO | g and Q clipped as above, no truncation (`fit_candidate.R:89-105`) | none |
| Diagnostic weights | No bound, so the largest weight can reach a million (`assess_design.R:1-7, 114-120`) | WeightIt `get_w_from_ps` does not truncate either |

| | Keep one policy in-house | Let each package bound its own inputs |
|---|---|---|
| Numerical results | Unchanged. | Four conventions would enter one estimate (a quantile trim in lmtp, a logit trim in SuperLearner, tmle's bounds), so candidates labeled by truncation level would mean different things. |
| Runtime and dependencies | None. | None. |
| Maintenance | One function and named constants. | Four conventions to follow across package versions. |

In the final-cohort operators comparison truncation is unlikely to matter. Only `final_operators_n_outside` = 2 of `final_operators_n` = 1,204 patients lie outside the 0.05 to 0.95 band, and in the outcome-free simulation the main-terms ATE bias at truncation 0.01 and 0.05 agrees to the sixth decimal (`writing/results-export/simulation_summary.csv`, the two ATE rows of the main-terms library, `max_abs_bias` 0.0105157 and 0.0105159). The Q bound differs between the ATE path (about 5e-4, inside tmle) and the ATO path (one in a million), which can matter for rare outcomes. My recommendation is to keep one in-house policy, name the constants once, document the ATO exception and the ATE-versus-ATT difference, and pin `length(gbound) == 2`. The diagnostic weights should be computed from the truncated score for each candidate or be labeled as untruncated.

Delegation changes nothing here, because every candidate already uses an influence-curve variance. The open question is the shortfall in the final-cohort operators comparison, where the model standard errors are 0.025 to 0.026 against empirical standard errors of 0.027 to 0.028 for the main-terms ATE (`final_operators_sim_main_terms_ate_se_mean_min`, `_se_mean_max`, `_emp_se_min`, `_emp_se_max`). I see two candidate causes outside the estimator. The plasmode world resamples rows with replacement (`simulate_design.R:92-100`), so a sample of n holds about 63% distinct rows while the variance formula treats it as n independent observations (inference). And the skill says that a noisy learner, or a fit that converges to the wrong function, can make the standard error reported under cross-fitting differ from the spread of the estimates (`references/three-draw-split.md`, the sections on a noisy learner and on a fit that converges to the wrong function). My recommendation is to keep them and to separate the two causes by running the coverage check of the ATO section with oracle nuisances, and once more on freshly drawn rows, which needs no change to the package.

## What must stay in-house, and why

The simulation (`simulate_design.R:112-115`), the negative-control ladder (`nc_ladder.R:229`) and the final estimate (`estimate_effect.R:180`) all call `fit_candidate()`, and `test-estimate-effect.R:4-21` checks that the analysis passes the dossier's candidate, folds and seed to it. This is what lets the dossier say that the estimator run after unblinding is the one the simulation evaluated, and it requires that nuisance fitting, cross-fitting and targeting sit behind one function that the package owns. The guarantee already has two stated exceptions, the comparators and the hazard models, and the paper should keep naming them. A second condition is not yet met, because the guarantee also depends on the numerical engines and the lock hash covers only the plan, the data, the covariates, the seed, K and the cleanTMLE version (`lock.R:17-23, 139`). Nothing records the versions of tmle, xgboost, glmnet, earth, nnet, WeightIt or MatchIt, although the manuscript reports them (`manuscript/methods.qmd:213`) and a different xgboost or glmnet can change the fitted values. `verify_lock()` warns on a cleanTMLE version mismatch (`lock.R:159-162`) and on nothing else.

Cross-fitting has to stay in-house for the same reason. tmle's own nuisance estimation is not the scheme the package uses (its g is fitted on all rows, and `cvQinit` applies to the outcome regression only; tmle 2.1.1 signature), and, as far as I know, `CV.SuperLearner` returns held-out predictions at the observed covariates only, so it could not give the predictions at A = 1 and A = 0 that `.fit_Q()` needs without saving every fold's fit (I did not read its source). SuperLearner cannot stratify folds when rows are grouped by `id`. The in-house `.cross_fit()` and `.make_folds()` keep copies of a bootstrapped row on one side of every split (`fit_candidate.R:13-29`, `learners.R:117-121`, `simulate_design.R:100-105`, `test-simulate-design.R:415`), and every seeded step runs under fixed generators (`utils.R:36-48`).

The edge check has to stay in-house because no package returns the held-out risk of every setting along a boosting or penalty path, and the skill's edge rule needs exactly that. It is incomplete in one respect that the open item makes urgent. `.cv_setting()` returns only `loss[k]`, the risk at the chosen point (`learners.R:138-146`), and `simulate_design()` returns the counts from `.edge_check()` and drops the per-setting risks (`simulate_design.R:390`), so the plateau test cannot be run afterwards.

The lock hashes stay in-house because no estimation package provides them. `.hash()` reduces any object, closures and their captured variables included, to a hash digest through `digest` (`utils.R:3-32`), and `.stamp()` and `.check_stamp()` bind each design object to the lock (`lock.R:187-201`). The only outside piece is `digest`.

## The thinnest architecture that keeps these guarantees

The package cannot become much thinner in the numerical layer without giving up one of the four guarantees, and I would not try. What can change is how much it claims to own and how well the owned parts are tied to the established ones.

| Layer | Today | Proposed |
|---|---|---|
| Plan, lock, dictionary, dossier, simulation, ladder, log, hashes | In-house | In-house; this is the contribution |
| Nuisance learners and super learner | In-house, untested against any package | In-house, pinned to `cv.glmnet`, `xgb.cv` and SuperLearner; engine versions recorded in the dossier |
| Targeting of ATE, trimmed ATE, ATT | tmle, no version floor | tmle (>= 2.1.1), pinned in both ATT regimes, refit reported |
| Targeting of ATO | In-house, one pin | In-house, pinned, coverage-checked, cited to [@lee2026] |
| Weighting, matching, balance | WeightIt, MatchIt, cobalt in Imports | Suggests, with a version guard; base R for the standardized difference |
| Time to event | concrete | concrete |
| Longitudinal and censoring (wishlist) | Outside the package | lmtp, run outside the design stage, as the case-study sensitivity analysis is |
| Naming the fitter | Fixed list of five learner names (`plan.R:2-3`) | After the paper: a fitter function in the plan, hashed as `select` already is (`utils.R:3-30`), so SuperLearner, sl3 or lmtp-style fitters can be candidates under the same guarantee |

The last row is the general form of the wishlist item "let comparators, learner libraries and seeds be [named] candidates" (`cleanTMLE/WISHLIST.md:44-45`). It would make the package a wrapper in the strict sense, with the in-house library as the default fitter, and it is the only route by which an external fitter could enter the design stage without breaking the shared-fitter guarantee. It is not needed for the paper. The plan-level `ps_library` is also the reason that both candidates in the case study share one propensity score (`plan.R:112-114, 170-171`; `fit_candidate.R:166`), which matters for the bias question below. None of these proposals changes the numbers of the design-stage runs now under way, except recommendations 6 and 7 if their experiments confirm a defect.

lmtp 1.5.4 is the right engine for the wishlist's longitudinal and censoring items and the wrong one for the point-treatment design stage, since it cannot target the ATT, the ATO or a trimmed population, it truncates differently, and it cannot accept externally fitted nuisances. sl3 adds the heaviest closure and is installed from GitHub on this machine. I would not make either a dependency of the core.

## The open learner-defaults item

The current library follows most of the skill. It has one learner type per family, with an elastic net, MARS, tuned boosting and a small neural net (`plan.R:3`; `test-learners.R:9-14`). The boosting grid is the skill's first grid exactly (depths 1 to 5, rates 0.1 and 0.3, cap 500), with `min_child_weight = 0`, one thread and the xgboost defaults otherwise (`learners.R:6-8, 77-79`). Every setting enters the combination separately on the same inner folds (`learners.R:123-146`), and the edge check reads the best setting of each type (`learners.R:228`). It departs from the skill in three ways. The edge check counts and does not run the plateau test, because neither the risk path nor its standard error is kept. The neural net's width is fixed at 5 without a stated reason, where the skill says width and decay both need tuning (`learners.R:11`). And the skill puts kernel ridge in the nets family below a thousand observations and a small net from a thousand up, while the training folds of the 1,204-patient comparison hold about 963 (1,204 times 0.8; K = 5), so most design-stage fits fall on the kernel side of that line. The combination is also computed from the same folds that chose each setting's round or penalty, which makes its risk optimistic; that is the nested scheme as the skill describes it and not a defect.

The design stage itself must use the nested scheme, because the estimator it assesses is the cross-fitted one. The experiments below concern learner settings and sources of bias, so the skill's three-draw split is the right tool for screening: each setting is fitted once on a training draw and scored on a validation draw, and the estimators run on a third draw, each draw the size of the analysis it stands for. That is 17 fits per nuisance where the nested scheme needs 510. The skill also requires that any claim about variance or coverage, and any estimator whose nuisance fits converge to a wrong function or are noisy, be re-run under the nested scheme at pilot scale. The bias and standard-error questions fall under that rule, and the grid questions do not. Only one R job may run at a time (`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-18-outcome-blind-design-stage.md:133`), so none of this can start until the design stage has finished. Each decision rule below should be written into the spec before the first run. Every experiment uses simulated data with a known truth, drawn from the simulation study's data-generating processes (`simulation/PLAN.md`, section 4) and from a wide variant with about 75 covariates, so that no default is tuned to the Rescue.Co results. Rescue.Co enters only through its outcome-free covariate matrix, in a final check that the chosen setting runs on the real design.

### Question 1: the decay grid of the neural net

The grid is 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1 at size 5, the fit has `maxit = 200` and a single random start that is the same for every decay at a given fold, and the fit's `convergence` flag is never read (`learners.R:11-12, 33, 66-75, 236-237`). The decay in nnet multiplies the sum of squared weights, and the data term is a sum over observations and not a mean (nnet 7.3-21, `src/nnet.c`, `fminfn`; 7.3-19 is installed), so the penalty per observation at a fixed decay is proportional to one over the training size. Training sets of about 181 (from `first_four_months_operators_n` = 226), 963 and about 5,200 (6,500 patients; `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-18-outcome-blind-design-stage.md:149`) span a 29-fold range, and one absolute grid cannot sit in the same place relative to the optimum in all three. The reported pattern, with the bottom of the grid chosen about two thirds of the time, the top about a third of the time and the middle almost never (`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-18-outcome-blind-design-stage.md:145`), is not what noise among three similar settings would produce, since the middle would win about a third of the time. I suspect unstable or unconverged fits (inference), and the experiment can tell that from a smooth risk curve.

The skill implies the plateau test, with each setting's validation risk and its standard error (the standard deviation of the held-out losses over the square root of their number). When the test fails it implies shifting the grid, adding settings past the edge that keeps winning and dropping the same number at the far end that is never chosen. It also implies asking whether the net earns its place, by comparing the combination's risk with and without it, and trying kernel ridge for training sizes below a thousand.

The smallest experiment fits `nnet` at size 5 on a log grid of six decays expressed per training observation (spaced by factors of ten from one in a million to one in ten, so that the absolute decay is that value times the training size), on the three-draw split, for the simulation study's near-linear and nonlinear outcome and treatment models with 4 covariates and with 75, at training sizes of 250, 1,000 and 5,000, with 100 data sets per cell. For each fit it records the validation log-loss of every decay with its standard error, the `convergence` flag and the number of iterations, and the log-loss excess over the true propensity score or outcome probability on a large test draw. It then applies the plateau test per cell. The decision rule is as follows. If the best decay per observation is stable across training sizes, replace the absolute grid by a per-observation grid centered there; if it is not, tune the decay on a grid that moves with n. If more than a tenth of the fits reach `maxit`, raise it or average two starts before judging the grid. If the combination's validation risk with the net is within one standard error of the risk without it, drop the net from the default library, which also removes the slowest per-fit learner (the profile in recommendation 8 confirms the cost). Width is examined only if the plateau test fails at size 5, by adding sizes 2 and 10. For the nets slot below a thousand observations, add a kernel ridge fit (or ridge on random Fourier features, if kernel ridge is too slow) as a rival and compare it on the same validation draws.

### Question 2: leaving the treatment unpenalized in the elastic net

`.fit_Q()` fits the outcome regression on `cbind(.trt = A, X)` (`fit_candidate.R:39`), and `.learner_fit()` calls `glmnet::glmnet(X, y, family = "binomial", alpha, lambda)` with no `penalty.factor` (`learners.R:65`), so the treatment coefficient is shrunk with the covariates on the standardized scale (`standardize = TRUE` is the glmnet default). In glmnet's documentation a `penalty.factor` of 0 means no shrinkage and that the variable is always in the model, and the factors are rescaled internally, which changes the penalty sequence (glmnet CRAN mirror source, `R/glmnet.R`; the installed version is 4.1-8 and the mirror is a later release). The path is computed by the same call in `.prepare_setting()` (`learners.R:43`), so the fix would have to give both calls the same factor. The other learners have no equivalent, since trees and nets treat `.trt` as one column among 76.

The skill implies that this is a fixed hyperparameter the analysis requires, to be recorded as fixed with its reason; my reading is that cross-validated log-loss for the outcome does not reward an accurate treatment contrast, so the tuned penalty has no reason to protect it. Two things are worth knowing before the experiment. Shrinking the treatment coefficient pulls the plug-in contrast toward zero, and every default surface has a positive effect (`log_or = 0.5`, `plan.R:239`), so shrinkage predicts a negative plug-in bias and not the positive bias seen for the super learner; leaving the coefficient free could therefore raise the super learner's bias. The decision rule must say beforehand that either result is reported. And fitting a separate outcome regression in each arm would avoid the question for every learner, but it would turn the main-terms candidate into a model with every treatment-by-covariate interaction, which changes what the case study compares, so it is a rival to test and not a drop-in.

The smallest experiment uses `glmnet` alone, with the true propensity score supplied, on the nonlinear surface with and without heterogeneity at effects of 0 and 0.5 on the log odds scale, 4 and 75 covariates, n of 250 and 1,000, and 500 data sets. The arms are the present call, the call with a penalty factor of 0 on `.trt`, and separate fits by arm. The outcomes are the bias of the plug-in contrast, the bias, standard-error ratio and coverage of the targeted ATE, and the validation log-loss. It runs in minutes. If a difference appears, repeat it once with the default super learner at pilot scale under the nested scheme.

### Question 3: the positive bias that both libraries share

The outcome-free design-stage output narrows the search. The main-terms bias is 0.011 for the ATE, 0.011 for the ATO, 0.010 for the ATT and 0.011 for the trimmed ATE (`final_operators_sim_main_terms_ate_worst_bias_min`, `_ato_worst_bias_max`, `_att_worst_bias_max`, `_trimmed_ate_worst_bias_max`), and 0.018 and 0.019 for the super learner on the ATE and the ATO (`final_operators_sim_super_learner_ate_worst_bias_min`, `_ato_worst_bias_max`). A bias that is nearly the same for four estimands, one of which has different targeting code, and unchanged by the truncation level, is unlikely to come from the targeting step or from truncation, which binds on 2 of 1,204 patients in the real design (`final_operators_n_outside`). What remains is common to all estimands: the initial outcome regression, the cross-fitted propensity score that both candidates share by construction (`plan.R:112-114, 170-171`), and the simulated world. Reading the code, I would add three features of the world to the list in the spec. The world's score is fitted on all rows and predicted on the same rows (`assess_design.R:103`, where the folds are all 1, and `fit_candidate.R:19-20` then trains and predicts on every row), so it is an in-sample fit of the whole library and can be more extreme and more complex than any honest estimate. The outcome surfaces use the logit of that score inside the prognostic index and as the effect modifier (`simulate_design.R:12-16, 25, 31`), so even the linear surface is linear in the covariates only to the extent that the score is. And the sample is a row bootstrap, so about a third of its rows are copies. None of this is a finding; each is something the experiment can test.

The skill implies the nested scheme for this question, since the nuisance fits may converge to the wrong function and the learners are noisy, with the three-draw split used for screening only where the two schemes agree.

The smallest experiment supplies the nuisances to the unchanged targeting step in stages, on a known-truth data-generating process with a smooth propensity score and, in a second world, on a package-style plasmode world built from the same covariates by `assess_design()`. In the first stage the true outcome regression and the true propensity score go straight to the targeting step (no learners, thousands of data sets in minutes). The bias must be zero, and a non-zero value is a defect in the truth or the targeting. In the next stages the true score is kept and the outcome regression is estimated, by main-terms regression and by the default library; then the outcome regression is true and the score is cross-fitted; then both are estimated, with K = 5 and with K = 1. Every stage records the plug-in bias before targeting, the bias after targeting, the ratio of the mean standard error to the empirical standard error, and coverage, at truncation 0.05 and with none. The cheap stages use a thousand data sets, and the stages that fit the default library use 100 under the nested scheme. A last pair of runs compares sampling rows with replacement against drawing fresh rows from the world, to see whether the standard-error shortfall belongs to the design. A cheap outcome-free check can come first and needs no simulation: compare the c-statistic and the share outside the 0.05 to 0.95 band of the world's score with those of the cross-fitted score, both held in the design object. The decision rule is that a source is named only if replacing it by the truth brings the bias within two Monte Carlo standard errors of zero. If no single stage does, the bias is the product of the outcome and propensity errors, and the paper rescopes the claim and leaves the design as it is.

### Question 4: the scope of the default surfaces

This is not a default change, and the smallest experiment is the one the spec already names. Add the simulation study's two outcome models outside the plan's family (a nonlinear prognosis with effect modification, and a threshold in the region of poor overlap; `simulation/PLAN.md`, section 4) as data-generating processes in the three experiments above, and read the main-terms and super learner biases on them. Nothing in the package or in the paper's scope statement needs to change before those results exist.

### Question 5: PC-HAL in place of the neural net

I agree with the spec's recommendation, and the skill gives the reason. PC-HAL, a principal-component version of the highly adaptive lasso (HAL), fits sums of the zero-order indicator basis through the leading principal components of the n-by-n kernel (`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-18-outcome-blind-design-stage.md:149`). The skill puts HAL of smoothness order 0 in the tree family, which boosting already covers, and puts the neural net in the family of smooth functions of all covariates at once, so replacing the net with PC-HAL would leave that family empty. The skill accepts a second learner within a family only for a property the first lacks, and HAL's convergence rate is such a property; that is the case for PC-HAL as an optional additional learner, not as a replacement. The repository (github.com/meixide/hapc, read on 2026-10-22) holds an R package with a C++ core built on Eigen, MIT-licensed, with no sign of a CRAN release on its page, so it could be a Suggests or Remotes entry and not an Import. Its examples use 100 observations and 5 covariates and state no limits. The two papers are not in `writing/references.bib`: [cite: PC-HAL, its convergence rate and its logistic-loss version]. The installed hal9001 0.4.6 (CRAN, 2023) already provides HAL with `smoothness_orders = 0`, `X_unpenalized` (which would also settle the unpenalized treatment for that learner) and a `foldid` argument, but its basis grows quickly with 75 covariates, so a method that works through the n-by-n kernel and not through the basis has an obvious appeal.

The smallest experiment runs on the simulation study's own data-generating processes, where n and d are small (n of 500 and 2,000, 4 covariates, all three outcome models). It fits three libraries on the three-draw split, namely the default, the default with PC-HAL added, and the default with the net replaced by PC-HAL, and compares validation log-loss for g and Q with standard errors, the combination's weight on PC-HAL, and the fit time per setting. The decision rule is to add PC-HAL as an optional learner only if it lowers the combination's validation risk by more than one standard error on the outside-the-family model and costs less than the net it would join; otherwise leave it out and say why in the supplement. It should not be run at the sizes of the larger Rescue.Co comparisons, where the n-by-n kernel is the reason the spec expects days of extra runtime.
